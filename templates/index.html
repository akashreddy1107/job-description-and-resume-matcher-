Why this analyzer is different

Plain-language differences from many “upload and chat” resume tools, with small examples.

1 Fairer signals — hide who they are from the AI

What “fairer” means here: You usually want to judge the work (skills, roles, results), not guess traits from a person’s name, email, or phone. This app removes that personal layer before the text is turned into search data, so the AI has less to latch onto besides job-related content.

Many other tools

The resume is often read and stored word-for-word. The model can still “see” names and contact lines next to skills. That is convenient, but it means the AI may pick up patterns you did not mean to use (for example, associations tied to how a name sounds or where an email domain looks like it is from).

This analyzer

Names, emails, and phone-like numbers are swapped for plain placeholders first. Only then is the text chunked and indexed. Questions and answers are grounded in that cleaned version, so the system is steered toward skills and history—not toward “who” they are on paper.

Example (before → after)
Original line on the resume:
“Alex Kim · alex.kim@company.org · +1 555-0100 · Led a team of 8 on a Python data pipeline.”
What gets stored for search (simplified):
“[REDACTED_PERSON] · [REDACTED_EMAIL] · [REDACTED_PHONE] · Led a team of 8 on a Python data pipeline.”
The important part for hiring—“led a team,” “Python,” “data pipeline”—stays. The name and contact routes are not there for the model to read.
Why this helps: You still get answers about skills and impact; you reduce the chance that the AI’s wording is colored by identity details that are not part of the job scorecard. (It is not perfect—always double-check—but the design pushes in a fairer direction.)

2 Smarter chunks (semantic chunking)

Typical tools

Some systems cut text every fixed number of characters. That can split a sentence or achievement in the middle.

This analyzer

Text is split mainly at paragraph boundaries, then packed into chunks up to a size limit. Whole ideas tend to stay together for search.

Example
Bad split: “… led a team at Go” | “ogle using Python.”
Our approach: keep the paragraph in one piece when possible, so “Google” and “Python” stay in the same retrieval context.

3 Multi-hop questions (two searches, one answer)

Typical tools

One search for your whole question. If “marketing” appears in one chunk and “Python” in another, the best match might be missed.

This analyzer

For chat Q&A, the system can split your question into sub-queries, search for each, merge the evidence, then answer—so separate facts can still be combined.

Example
You ask: “Do they have marketing experience and Python?”
Behind the scenes: Sub-search A pulls “marketing” passages; sub-search B pulls “Python” passages; the answer uses both. A single combined search might return neither chunk as the top hit.

4 Job-description fit (structured output)

Typical tools

You might paste a JD into a chat and get a free-form paragraph. Easy to read, but not always consistent across candidates.

This analyzer

Automated Contextual Fit pulls relevant resume text against the JD and asks for a structured review: strengths, gaps, and missing information—so comparisons are easier.

Example
You paste a JD that requires “Kubernetes + 5 years backend.”
What you get: bullets for what matches, what’s unclear, and what’s missing—instead of only a one-line “looks good.”

Many products on the web share similar ideas; this app implements the pipeline above in this workspace. Always verify results before hiring decisions.
